Chamomile

Matricaria chamomilla

Small, daisy-like flowers that have been dried and steeped as a calming tea across European and Middle Eastern traditions for centuries, often reached for in the evening as part of a wind-down routine.

Traditional Use — Evening tea, aromatic
Specimen 02 · VegaGoStarts

Peppermint

Mentha × piperita

A natural hybrid of two mint species, valued historically for its sharp, cooling aroma. Long used across many cultures as a digestive tea after meals, and widely grown simply for its culinary versatility.

Traditional Use — After-meal tea, culinary
Specimen 03 · VegaGoStarts

Ginger

Zingiber officinale

A flowering plant grown for its underground stem, or rhizome, used in cooking and traditional practices across Asia for thousands of years — one of the oldest and most widely traded spices in human history.

Traditional Use — Culinary, warming tea
Specimen 04 · VegaGoStarts

Lavender

Lavandula angustifolia

Named from the Latin lavare, "to wash," reflecting its long use in bathing and laundry across Roman history. Its dried buds remain a common addition to sachets and calming blends today.

Traditional Use — Aromatic, sachets
VegaGoStarts Field Notes

Where Herbal Tradition and Modern Kitchens Overlap

A surprising number of herbs now considered purely culinary — mint, ginger, thyme, sage — spent centuries doing double duty in traditional herbal practice long before they became kitchen staples, as VegaGoStarts has traced through several older household records. The line between "cooking herb" and "medicinal herb" is, historically, a fairly recent and somewhat artificial one; in most older traditions, a well-stocked kitchen and a well-stocked apothecary shelf overlapped substantially.

Much of this knowledge developed the way most practical knowledge did before modern chemistry: through generations of careful observation, passed down through households rather than written into formal texts. Some of it has since been studied more formally; a great deal of it remains primarily cultural and historical rather than clinically established, a distinction VegaGoStarts is careful to keep front and center.

That distinction matters. Enjoying chamomile tea for its taste and its long cultural association with rest is very different from treating any herb as a guaranteed remedy for a specific condition. The richest way to engage with this history is often the simplest — as a genuinely interesting record of what people reached for, and why, long before pharmacies existed, which is exactly the spirit VegaGoStarts brings to this collection.
